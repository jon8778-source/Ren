**[S1: 0:00-0:35]**

Maya saved twelve dollars last night. She canceled a subscription she barely used, checked the confirmation email, and felt pretty good about it. Then she opened her work benefits portal. There was a number sitting in there that made the subscription look tiny. One hundred dollars a month. Twelve hundred dollars a year. Her employer was willing to put that into her retirement account, but only if she made one change. She'd worked there for three years and never noticed. This isn't a story about a secret investment trick. It's about a benefit hiding in plain sight.

**[S2: 0:35-1:18]**

Meet Maya. She's thirty-one, earns sixty thousand dollars a year, and has a pretty normal relationship with money. She pays her bills, puts something aside when she can, and tries not to think about how expensive groceries have become. When she joined her company, she ticked the box to contribute two percent to her four-oh-one-k. The form said she'd been enrolled, so she moved on. So how did she end up staring at that number? It started earlier that week, with a message from HR: Review your retirement contributions. She nearly deletes it. Then Alex, a colleague, stops by her desk and points to a line she's never read: Employer match: one hundred percent, up to four percent of eligible pay.

**[S3: 1:18-2:09]**

So what does that mean? It means that in this example, for every dollar Maya contributes, her company adds a dollar, until she contributes four percent of her salary. On sixty thousand dollars, four percent is twenty-four hundred a year. That's two hundred dollars per month from Maya, and up to another two hundred from her employer. But Maya's been putting in two percent. That's twelve hundred a year from her, with twelve hundred matched by work. She hasn't been getting nothing. She's been getting half the match she could receive. The missing half is another twelve hundred dollars a year. That's one hundred a month of employer money, without asking for a raise or working an extra shift. The catch is that Maya has to put in her own extra hundred a month too, and qualify under her plan.

**[S4: 2:09-2:57]**

Maya's first thought is entirely reasonable: I can't just find another hundred dollars every month. Rent doesn't disappear because a spreadsheet looks exciting. And this is where some videos would shout, It's free money, do it now! But it's not quite that simple. To unlock the extra match, Maya would contribute an extra hundred dollars a month from her gross salary. If she uses traditional pre-tax contributions, the reduction in her take-home pay may be less than a hundred, because her taxable income changes. If she uses Roth contributions, there's no upfront tax break, so her take-home pay falls by the full hundred. And no, an employer match is not cash she can spend next weekend. It's money in a retirement account, invested under her plan's options, with rules about when she can access it.

**[S5: 2:57-3:40]**

Now Maya has a choice, not an order. If she's struggling to cover necessities, or she's carrying expensive debt, she shouldn't pretend those problems don't exist. But she checks her budget instead of guessing. The subscription was twelve dollars. A delivery habit she'd already wanted to cut is another forty. A few unused app plans and a gym membership she never visits cover the other forty-eight. That adds up to a hundred dollars a month. Even if her take-home pay fell by the full hundred, she could cover it without draining her emergency savings. She opens the four-oh-one-k contribution page, changes two percent to four, then pauses before pressing save. Because there is one more line she needs to read.

**[S6: 3:40-4:28]**

Vesting. It's an unglamorous word that can make a very real difference. Maya always owns the money she contributes herself. Her employer's money may become fully hers immediately, or only after she meets her plan's service requirements. Different employers use different rules. The match formula can vary, too. Some match fifty cents per dollar. Some set different percentages. Some don't offer a match at all. Eligibility dates, pay-period calculations and year-end true-ups can all matter. A true-up tops up the match if her contributions were uneven across the year. That's why copying a percentage from a stranger online isn't a plan. Maya finds the Summary Plan Description and checks what her employer actually promises. In this example, employer contributions vest after three years of service, and she's already reached that mark.

**[S7: 4:28-5:02]**

Satisfied with what she's read, Maya presses save. Then she does three things. First, she keeps the confirmation page, so there's a record of what she chose and when. Second, once the change reaches payroll, she checks her next pay stub to see the new contribution. Third, she checks her four-oh-one-k statement to see the employer match arriving beside it. A few weeks later, it all appears. Her paycheck is a little smaller. Her retirement deposit is bigger. And she's no longer wondering whether she missed a box in a benefits form. She has evidence.

**[S8: 5:02-6:00]**

Here's the part Maya wishes she'd understood earlier. The one hundred dollars a month she wasn't getting wasn't a guaranteed investment return. It wasn't a gift card. It was an employer contribution she was eligible to unlock under this example plan. In the three years she's already worked there, that's about thirty-six hundred dollars left unclaimed. Leave it unclaimed for ten more years, and that's twelve thousand dollars of employer contributions alone, before considering any investment growth, changes in salary, or changes to the plan. Markets can rise or fall. The match is still worth understanding. So before you spend another evening hunting for tiny savings, give your retirement benefits ten minutes.
