## Scene 1

Every November, my work email sends me the same message: "Open enrollment closes Friday." And every November, I click "Keep my current plan" in about four seconds. This year, I spent forty minutes on it instead. Those forty minutes turned out to be worth almost two thousand dollars a year.

## Scene 2

Quick catch-up if you're new. I'm Ren. At 25, I leased a shiny new car so people would think I was doing well. At 28, I handed it back, bought a used one, and survived a Christmas that left me with eighty-five dollars in the bank. That January, I audited every monthly payment I had. Seven hundred and fifty dollars a month, cut to four hundred and forty-five. I was proud of that audit. But I missed one. The payment that leaves before my paycheck even shows up: my health insurance premium. I never audited it because I never saw it. It was just a line on a pay stub, sitting between federal tax and something I still can't explain. I'm still driving that same used car, by the way. Eight years old now. I earn around a hundred thousand. And I was still auto-renewing a health plan I'd picked in four seconds, years ago.

## Scene 3

This year, the email had a new line: "Your premium contribution will increase." And it's not just my company. A big benefits survey from Marsh found employers expect health-benefit costs per worker to rise about eight point two percent in 2027, the biggest jump since 2003. About two-thirds of large employers said they'd ask workers to pay a bigger share. My plan went from a hundred and eighty-five dollars a month to two hundred and ten. That's two thousand five hundred and twenty dollars a year, just for the right to use insurance. So I did what everyone does. I asked the group chat. Alex said, "Get the best plan. It's your health, man." Alex also buys a new car every three years, so "best" is kind of his default setting. Danny said, "Get the cheapest. You never go to the doctor." Danny also financed a truck with a payment bigger than my rent. And Uncle Gary replied, "I don't even look. I just pay it." Then he sent a photo of his boat. Three opinions. Zero numbers.

## Scene 4

So I walked next door to Dev. Dev's my neighbor. He's driven the same sedan since before I could legally drive, and he never gives advice. He just asks three questions. He looked at my two options and asked: "What's the most each plan can cost you in a really bad year? What did you actually use last year? And where does the difference go?" Question one, the bad year. The PPO: twenty-five twenty in premiums, plus an out-of-pocket maximum of four thousand. Worst case, six thousand five hundred and twenty. The high-deductible plan was just seventy dollars a month, so eight hundred and forty a year. But its out-of-pocket max is six thousand five hundred. My employer also puts seven hundred and fifty dollars into a health savings account if I pick it. Worst case: six thousand five hundred and ninety. In a disaster year, the plans were basically tied. Seventy dollars apart. Question two, what did I actually use? I pulled my claims. One checkup, which was free on both plans, a couple of urgent care visits, and a prescription. About six hundred dollars of care. On the PPO, premiums plus copays came to roughly two thousand six hundred and seventy. On the high-deductible plan, I'd pay the six hundred myself, plus eight-forty in premiums, minus the seven-fifty from my employer. About six hundred and ninety. Same doctor. Same me. Almost two thousand dollars of difference.

## Scene 5

Question three was the one I didn't understand. Where does the difference go? So I texted my cousin Priya. Priya earns about two hundred thousand a year and you would never guess it. She drives a hatchback and wears the same fleece to every family event. She wrote back one sentence: "Pay the difference to yourself." Here's what she meant. A health savings account is money you put in before tax. If it's invested, it can grow without being taxed. And when you spend it on qualified medical costs, it comes out tax-free. For 2027, the IRS limit for self-only coverage is four thousand five hundred dollars. So I picked the high-deductible plan, and I moved the hundred and forty dollars a month I wasn't paying in premiums straight into the HSA. Sixteen hundred and eighty a year, plus my employer's seven-fifty. Two thousand four hundred and thirty dollars by the end of year one. That's not cheaper insurance. That's my own medical cushion, built from money I was already spending.

## Scene 6

Now, this is not a "high deductible always wins" video. If you're expecting a baby, planning a surgery, or managing a condition with regular specialist visits or expensive prescriptions, the PPO might be the better deal for you. Check that your doctors are in-network. Check the drug list. Run the bad-year number and the normal-year number. And if you buy your own coverage on the marketplace, this is your season too. Open enrollment started November first, and in most states you need to pick by December fifteenth for coverage that starts January first. KFF says benchmark silver premiums are up about thirteen percent on average for 2027. One health policy analyst put it simply: automatically renewing before you look at alternatives is a big mistake. That was me. Four years running.

## Scene 7

So here's what I learned. Spending better doesn't always mean spending less. Sometimes the expensive plan is exactly right. The mistake isn't picking the wrong plan. It's not picking at all. Three questions: the bad year, the real year, and where the difference goes. If this helped, stick around, because the next video is about the other box on that same benefits page. The one I ignored for three years. It cost me money I can never get back. Forty minutes, once a year. Cheapest raise I ever gave myself.
