## Scene 1 — Four seconds versus forty minutes (0:00–0:20)
**Visual:** Home office; inbox with “Open enrollment closes Friday”; Ren's cursor reaches “Keep current plan”. A four-second stopwatch changes into a forty-minute timer. A large “$1,980 potential difference” card appears. Small opening disclaimer.

**Narration:**
Every November, the same email arrives: “Open enrollment closes Friday.” Four years running, I clicked “Keep my current plan” in about four seconds. This year, I spent forty minutes checking the numbers instead. My normal-year comparison revealed a potential difference of one thousand nine hundred and eighty dollars. And I nearly clicked straight past it.

## Scene 2 — The bill I couldn't see (0:20–1:00)
**Visual:** Ren at 25 with leased-car silhouette; at 28 with used-car keys; illustrated bank balance $85; payment audit $750 → $445. Cut to pay stub medical-premium line. Keep mostly motion-graphics rather than new unapproved character art.

**Narration:**
I'm Ren. At twenty-five, I leased a shiny car because I wanted people to think I was doing well. By twenty-eight, I'd handed back the keys, bought a used one and survived a Christmas that left me with eighty-five dollars in my bank account. That January, I audited my bills and cut monthly payments from seven hundred and fifty dollars to four hundred and forty-five. I'm still driving that used car, eight years later. But I missed one expense. It disappeared before my paycheck arrived: the health-insurance premium. I'd never questioned it.

## Scene 3 — Three opinions, zero calculations (1:00–1:45)
**Visual:** Email “Your premium contribution will increase”; $185 → $210/month; $2,520/year. A single sourced Marsh 2027 graphic. Group text-message bubbles from Alex, Danny and Uncle Gary, with the boat appearing as a tiny icon, not a new unsupported character portrait.

**Narration:**
Then this year's email added a sentence: “Your premium contribution will increase.” Mine was going from one hundred and eighty-five to two hundred and ten dollars a month. That's two thousand five hundred and twenty dollars a year, before a single doctor's bill. And the pressure is wider than my office: Marsh projects that employers' average health-benefit costs per worker will rise eight point two percent in 2027. That's an employer-cost forecast, not an eight-point-two-percent increase to every person's premium. I asked the group chat. Alex said, “Pick the best.” Danny said, “Pick the cheapest.” Uncle Gary said, “I never look,” and sent a picture of his boat. Three opinions. Zero calculations. So I went next door.

## Scene 4 — Dev asks three questions (1:45–2:55)
**Visual:** Porch/old sedan establishing still, then focus on Dev's three graphic question cards. Two-plan comparison builds line by line. A simple silhouette or off-screen dialogue is acceptable until a matching Dev design is approved.

**Narration:**
My neighbor Dev drives an old sedan and almost never gives advice. He asks questions. Three, this time: “What's the most this could cost in a bad year? What would it have cost in your actual year? And where would the difference go?”

We opened the plan summaries. These are illustrative numbers, not a quote for your coverage.

Plan A, a PPO: two hundred and ten dollars a month in premiums, or two thousand five hundred and twenty a year. Add its four-thousand-dollar in-network out-of-pocket maximum. In a bad year of covered in-network care, that's six thousand five hundred and twenty.

Plan B, an HSA-eligible high-deductible plan: seventy dollars a month, or eight hundred and forty a year. Its in-network out-of-pocket maximum was six thousand five hundred. So the total before the employer contribution would be seven thousand three hundred and forty. My employer would also add seven hundred and fifty to my HSA. After counting that contribution as value, the net comparison comes to six thousand five hundred and ninety. Just seventy dollars apart. But I'd need enough cash to manage the higher deductible, and neither figure protects me from every uncovered or out-of-network bill.

## Scene 5 — The normal-year comparison (2:55–3:50)
**Visual:** Use a clean table, no number-counting over completed equations. PPO: premium $2,520 + copays $150 = $2,670. HDHP: premium $840 + care paid $600 = $1,440 cash outflow; employer HSA $750 value; illustrative net economic cost $690. Fixed difference $1,980. Show “Employer HSA value ≠ immediate cash discount.”

**Narration:**
Then Dev asked what I had actually used. Say my covered, in-network care was six hundred dollars, and under this example's rules I'd pay one hundred and fifty in PPO copays. That makes the PPO two thousand six hundred and seventy dollars for the year, including premiums.

On the high-deductible plan, I'd pay eight hundred and forty in premiums plus six hundred for care: fourteen hundred and forty dollars out of pocket. The employer's seven-hundred-and-fifty-dollar HSA contribution is an additional benefit, bringing the illustrative net economic cost to six hundred and ninety. Difference: one thousand nine hundred and eighty dollars. It isn't an instant cash rebate or a guaranteed saving. It depends on the services, negotiated prices, prescriptions and network. But now I had an actual comparison, not a feeling about which name sounded safer.

## Scene 6 — Priya's HSA lesson (3:50–4:50)
**Visual:** Text from Priya: “Pay the difference to yourself.” Three arrows: $140/month → $1,680 personal HSA contributions + $750 employer = $2,430 total contributed, before spending. Show split: after $600 HSA withdrawal, $1,830 remains; if expenses are paid outside HSA, $2,430 remains. Flag pre-tax, eligibility and annual limits in small accurate captions.

**Narration:**
The third question was the one I hadn't considered: where does the difference go? I texted my cousin Priya. She drives a little hatchback, wears the same fleece everywhere and replied, “Pay the difference to yourself.”

The premium gap was a hundred and forty dollars a month. If I could afford it, I could put that money into an HSA instead: sixteen hundred and eighty dollars over a year. Add the employer's seven hundred and fifty, and that's two thousand four hundred and thirty dollars contributed before spending or growth. If I used six hundred of the HSA to pay medical bills, eighteen hundred and thirty would remain. If I paid those bills from other cash, the full two thousand four hundred and thirty could stay invested or saved.

An HSA has federal tax advantages when you're eligible, and qualified medical withdrawals can be tax-free. For 2027, the self-only contribution limit is four thousand five hundred dollars, including employer money. State treatment and eligibility rules matter. The point wasn't to make medical costs disappear. It was to give the money a job.

## Scene 7 — When the PPO might win (4:50–5:35)
**Visual:** Balanced risk checklist, doctor network card, formulary, expensive prescription icon, family calendar. Small Marketplace information panel kept separate from employer enrollment. No medical fear-mongering.

**Narration:**
And this is where the story needs a warning label. The high-deductible plan doesn't always win. If you're expecting a baby, planning surgery, seeing specialists regularly or taking expensive medication, the PPO could be much better. Check both networks, your drug coverage, the exact deductible and the out-of-pocket limits. Remember that high expenses can arrive before your HSA has much money in it.

If you shop through the health-insurance Marketplace rather than work, that's a separate enrollment process. For 2027, HealthCare.gov shows a December fifteenth deadline for January-first coverage and January fifteenth as the final open-enrollment date. State rules can differ. Your employer's deadline might be much sooner.

## Scene 8 — The choice (5:35–6:10)
**Visual:** Return to approved left-desk office layout. Three question cards collapse into a checklist. Calm final selection rather than a dramatic “winner”. Short tease of another box on the benefits page without specifying unverified details. Small closing disclaimer.

**Narration:**
Back at my laptop, I didn't click “Keep my current plan.” I checked my own numbers and made a deliberate choice. Not because cheaper premiums always mean cheaper healthcare. They don't. I learned to ask: what's the bad year, what's the real year, and where would the difference go? Forty minutes once a year. That's a better habit than four seconds of autopilot.

Next time, there's another box on the benefits page I ignored for years. And the money I missed can't be recovered. If this was useful, stick around.
