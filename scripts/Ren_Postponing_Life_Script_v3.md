# What If You Keep Postponing the Life You’re Saving For?

**Ren — story-led narration, v3 (Alex only)**

**Runtime target:** about 6½–7 minutes. Time the final voice track before production.

**Cast:** Ren narrates throughout and reads Alex’s lines. Alex is the only character, and he is alone in every scene. A friend’s message appears only as text on a phone screen. No new character designs.

**On-screen, not spoken:** “Example only, not financial advice” small caption whenever the budget appears. Speaker tag “Alex:” as a small caption whenever Ren reads one of Alex’s quoted lines.

**Section markers** (S1, S2 and so on) are for the shot plan only. They are not spoken.

---

[S1 — Hook]

Alex has a photo of a mountain, taped to his fridge.

He’s never been there.

But he’s researched the cabins, compared the routes, and watched a man on YouTube explain which hiking socks will apparently change his life.

He’s been ready for four years.

Every year, Alex says the same thing.

“Next year. I’ll be in a better position.”

And every year, he has a reason.

The uncomfortable part?

Some of those reasons are very good.

I’m Ren. And this is a story about what happens when you keep postponing the life you’re saving for.

[S2 — Waiting that helped]

When that photo first went up, his car had just broken down.

He had credit card debt, very little savings, and a vacation budget that was mostly optimism.

So he waited.

He paid down the card. Built up emergency savings. Started putting money away for retirement.

That was progress. Waiting helped.

[S3 — The moving summit]

Then the trip came up again.

Alex opened his accounts.

He could afford it now. But he’d just hit a savings milestone, and spending any of it felt like going backward.

“Let me get a little more comfortable first.”

So the photo stayed on the fridge.

Another year passed. Alex got a raise.

Then he raised his savings target.

The new target wasn’t unreasonable. Neither was the one after that.

But something was happening.

Whenever his finances improved, the definition of “ready” improved with them.

His mountain had developed a moving summit.

[S4 — The question]

One evening, he caught himself looking at cabins again.

Same website. Same mountains. Different prices.

So he asked himself, “What would have to happen for me to actually book this?”

He started listing numbers.

Then he stopped.

“And if I reach those numbers, will I feel comfortable spending it?”

He didn’t have an answer straight away.

Because he wasn’t sure.

[S5 — Ren steps out of the story]

Let’s pause his story here.

Alex isn’t foolish. He remembers when a car repair became a crisis, and money in the bank means options.

But he’s started treating every planned expense like the start of another emergency.

He knows how to save for the trip.

He hasn’t worked out how to give himself permission to take it.

And there’s something else.

The trip itself has grown.

Two nights became a week. A simple cabin became the beautiful one with the huge windows.

He wanted it to feel worth the wait.

But the bigger it gets, the more reluctant he becomes.

Without meaning to, he’s making it harder to say yes.

[S6 — The smaller version]

Then a friend sends him a picture.

A muddy trail. A sandwich. A view.

“You’d love this. Only a couple of hours away.”

Alex’s first thought is, “It’s not really the trip I planned.”

His second thought is, “I know. But what part am I waiting for?”

What he actually wants is a few days outside. Breakfast without checking work messages. Time that doesn’t involve deciding what needs fixing in the house.

The expensive cabin would be lovely.

But the things he wanted most came in a smaller size.

[S7 — The plan]

So he sits down with his budget.

This is the less cinematic part of the story. Nobody runs to the airport.

There’s a spreadsheet.

Alex is delighted.

He checks his bills and goals, and settles on an amount he can spend without borrowing or touching the emergency fund.

Then he prices the whole weekend. Somewhere to stay. Gas. Food. Park fees. A little extra for whatever he’s forgotten.

Two nights nearby. Packed lunches. No fancy cabin.

There’s still a trade-off. That money won’t be saved or invested.

He decides the weekend is worth that cost.

[S8 — Clicking book]

Then the hardest part.

Alex clicks “book.”

He feels excited for about three seconds.

Then he thinks about canceling.

He doesn’t tell himself he’s being ridiculous. He just looks at the plan again.

Bills covered. Savings on track. Nothing has changed in five minutes.

The discomfort is real.

It just isn’t evidence that booking was a mistake.

[S9 — If there’s no room]

Now, if your budget has no room right now, your version of this story looks different.

You might be in the part where Alex needed to wait.

That deserves respect.

A local walk, a free event, or an afternoon with someone you care about can still give you something to look forward to.

[S10 — The weekend]

Alex finally takes his weekend.

It rains on the first morning.

He forgets the coffee.

He takes a photo of himself staring into an empty mug like he’s just received terrible financial news.

He walks. He gets briefly lost. He eats a slightly flattened sandwich.

And he thinks about something other than work, bills, or when he’ll finally go away.

[S11 — The second photo]

When he gets home, the mountain photo stays on the fridge. He still wants the bigger trip.

But next to it, he puts a photo from his weekend.

He has mud on his jeans. He doesn’t look particularly glamorous.

He looks happy.

A month later, he reviews his finances. Some things still need attention. One weekend didn’t fix everything.

But he stuck to the plan.

And Alex has something he didn’t have before: proof that he can spend on purpose and still be a responsible saver.

[S12 — Takeaway and close]

So here’s my challenge.

Pick one thing you keep postponing.

Ask what you actually want from it.

Then find a version that fits your money, your time, and your life as it is now.

Maybe you book it. Maybe you set a date and start saving toward it. Maybe you make the phone call that costs nothing.

Just give “someday” a next step.

Alex’s fridge used to show him a place he wanted to go.

Now it also shows him somewhere he’s been.

What’s still sitting on your fridge? Tell me in the comments.

I’m Ren. Subscribe for more on building wealth and making room for the life it’s meant to support.

## Production notes — not spoken

- The fridge photo is the visual anchor: aspiration at the open, postponement in the middle, a real memory beside it at the end. Return to it at S1, S3, S11 and S12.
- Pilot = S1 + S2 (≈145 words, ≈60 seconds).
- Alex is never the villain or foolish. His caution comes from a real past emergency.
- The budget is hypothetical. Show categories only, with no dollar amounts. No universal spending allowance or guaranteed outcome.
- This script does not authorise full production. A 60-second pilot must be approved by Jon first.

## Changes from v2

- Alex is the only character, and he is alone throughout. Maya is removed.
- Maya’s lines now belong to Alex. In S4 he asks himself her two questions. In S6 his “first thought” and “second thought” replace their exchange.
- S5: the growing trip is now Alex’s own habit (“there’s something else”), and the close is “he’s making it harder to say yes”.
- S6: the friend’s message stays as text on Alex’s phone; the friend never appears.
- S10–S11: Alex takes the empty-mug photo of himself, and puts the weekend photo up himself. “They both look happy” becomes “He looks happy”.
- Everything else is unchanged apart from pronouns (they → he, their → his, we → I).
