# What If You Keep Postponing the Life You’re Saving For?

**Ren — story-led narration, v2 (tightened)**

**Runtime target:** about 6½ minutes (~980 words at ~150 wpm with natural pauses). Time the final voice track before production.

**Cast:** Ren narrates throughout and reads Alex’s and Maya’s lines. Alex uses the approved Alex stills. Maya has no approved design, so she stays off-screen or is implied (her hand, her phone, her photos). No new character designs.

**On-screen, not spoken:** “Example only, not financial advice” small caption whenever the budget appears. Speaker tags (“Alex:” / “Maya:”) as small captions whenever Ren reads a quoted line.

**Section markers** (S1, S2 and so on) are for the shot plan only. They are not spoken.

---

[S1 — Hook]

Alex and Maya have a photo of a mountain taped to their fridge.

They’ve never been there.

But they’ve researched the cabins, compared the routes, and watched a man on YouTube explain which hiking socks will apparently change their lives.

They’ve been ready for four years.

Every year, Alex says the same thing.

“Next year. We’ll be in a better position.”

And every year, he has a reason.

The uncomfortable part?

Some of those reasons are very good.

I’m Ren. And this is a story about what happens when you keep postponing the life you’re saving for.

[S2 — Waiting that helped]

When that photo first went up, their car had just broken down.

They had credit card debt, very little savings, and a vacation budget that was mostly optimism.

So they waited.

They paid down the card. Built up emergency savings. Started putting money away for retirement.

That was progress. Waiting helped.

[S3 — The moving summit]

Then Maya brought up the trip again.

Alex opened their accounts.

They could afford it now. But he’d just hit a savings milestone, and spending any of it felt like going backward.

“Let’s get a little more comfortable first.”

Maya understood. She wanted security too.

So the photo stayed on the fridge.

Another year passed. Alex got a raise.

Then he raised their savings target.

The new target wasn’t unreasonable. Neither was the one after that.

But Maya noticed something.

Whenever their finances improved, the definition of “ready” improved with them.

Their mountain had developed a moving summit.

[S4 — Maya’s question]

One evening, she found Alex looking at cabins again.

Same website. Same mountains. Different prices.

Maya asked him, “What would have to happen for us to actually book this?”

Alex started listing numbers.

She stopped him.

“And if we reach those numbers, will you feel comfortable spending it?”

He didn’t answer straight away.

Because he wasn’t sure.

[S5 — Ren steps out of the story]

Let’s pause their story here.

Alex isn’t foolish. He remembers when a car repair became a crisis, and money in the bank means options.

But he’s started treating every planned expense like the start of another emergency.

He knows how to save for the trip.

He hasn’t worked out how to give himself permission to take it.

And Maya’s part in this?

The trip she keeps suggesting has grown.

Two nights became a week. A simple cabin became the beautiful one with the huge windows.

She wanted it to feel worth the wait.

But the bigger it gets, the more reluctant Alex becomes.

Without meaning to, they’re helping each other postpone it.

[S6 — The smaller version]

Then a friend sends them a picture.

A muddy trail. A sandwich. A view.

“You two would love this. Only a couple of hours away.”

Maya shows Alex. “What if we did this instead?”

Alex says, “It’s not really the trip we planned.”

And Maya says, “I know. But what part are we waiting for?”

What they actually want is a few days outside. Breakfast without checking work messages. Time together that doesn’t involve deciding what needs fixing in the house.

The expensive cabin would be lovely.

But the things they wanted most came in a smaller size.

[S7 — The plan]

So they sit down with their budget.

This is the less cinematic part of the story. Nobody runs to the airport.

There’s a spreadsheet.

Alex is delighted.

They check their bills and goals, and agree on an amount they can spend without borrowing or touching the emergency fund.

Then they price the whole weekend. Somewhere to stay. Gas. Food. Park fees. A little extra for whatever they’ve forgotten.

Two nights nearby. Packed lunches. No fancy cabin.

There’s still a trade-off. That money won’t be saved or invested.

They decide the weekend is worth that cost.

[S8 — Clicking book]

Then the hardest part.

Alex clicks “book.”

He feels excited for about three seconds.

Then he thinks about canceling.

Maya doesn’t tell him he’s being ridiculous. They just look at the plan again.

Bills covered. Savings on track. Nothing has changed in five minutes.

The discomfort is real.

It just isn’t evidence that booking was a mistake.

[S9 — If there’s no room]

Now, if your budget has no room right now, your version of this story looks different.

You might be in the part where Alex and Maya needed to wait.

That deserves respect.

A local walk, a free event, or an afternoon with someone you care about can still give you something to look forward to.

[S10 — The weekend]

Alex and Maya finally take their weekend.

It rains on the first morning.

Alex forgets the coffee.

Maya takes a photo of him staring into an empty mug like he’s just received terrible financial news.

They walk. They get briefly lost. They eat slightly flattened sandwiches.

And they talk about something other than work, bills, or when they’ll finally go away.

[S11 — The second photo]

When they get home, the mountain photo stays on the fridge. They still want the bigger trip.

But next to it, Maya puts a photo from their weekend.

Alex has mud on his jeans. Neither of them looks particularly glamorous.

They both look happy.

A month later, they review their finances. Some things still need attention. One weekend didn’t fix everything.

But they stuck to the plan.

And Alex has something he didn’t have before: proof that he can spend on purpose and still be a responsible saver.

[S12 — Takeaway and close]

So here’s my challenge.

Pick one thing you keep postponing.

Ask what you actually want from it.

Then find a version that fits your money, your time, and your life as it is now.

Maybe you book it. Maybe you set a date and start saving toward it. Maybe you make the phone call that costs nothing.

Just give “someday” a next step.

Alex and Maya’s fridge used to show them a place they wanted to go.

Now it also shows them somewhere they’ve been.

What’s still sitting on your fridge? Tell me in the comments.

I’m Ren. Subscribe for more on building wealth and making room for the life it’s meant to support.

## Production notes — not spoken

- The fridge photo is the visual anchor: aspiration at the open, postponement in the middle, a real memory beside it at the end. Return to it at S1, S3, S11 and S12.
- Pilot = S1 + S2 (~150 words, ≈60 seconds).
- Neither Alex nor Maya is the villain. Both have understandable priorities.
- The budget is hypothetical. Show categories only, with no dollar amounts, unless clearly labelled as an example. No universal spending allowance or guaranteed outcome.
- This script does not authorise full production. A 60-second pilot must be approved by Jon first.
