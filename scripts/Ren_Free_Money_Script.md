
**[S1: Cold open]**

I was 28 when Dev held up my pay stub and asked me one question. "Who's keeping the difference?" I didn't know what he meant. Then he pointed at one line, and my stomach dropped. For three years, I'd been turning down free money. And the worst part? I did it to impress people I barely knew. Let me take you back.

**[S2: Flashback, age 25]**

Twenty-five. First real job. Forty-four thousand a year, and I felt like I'd made it. So I did what felt right and leased a brand-new car, four hundred and eighty-nine dollars a month. Not because I needed it. Because I wanted everyone in that parking lot to think I was doing great. On day one, HR gave me a folder. Somewhere on page six it said the company would match every dollar I put into my retirement plan, up to four percent of my salary. I never got to page six. I picked two percent, because two sounded responsible, and the lease sounded urgent.

**[S3: The quiet years]**

Here's the thing about a match. If I put in four percent, they put in four percent, dollar for dollar. I put in two, so they put in two. The other two percent? It wasn't saved for later. It just never got paid. That was eight hundred and eighty dollars the first year. Then I got small raises, forty-six thousand, forty-eight, and every year I left the same two percent, and every year the ghost money got bigger. Meanwhile, I made that lease payment on time, every month, with pride. Four hundred eighty-nine dollars to look successful, while skipping about seventy-seven a month that would have actually made me successful.

**[S4: Back on the porch]**

Back on that porch, Dev asked me three questions. "What does this line mean? What would it say if you'd read the folder? And who's keeping the difference?" The line said two percent. It should have said four. And the difference? The company keeps it. Not because they're evil. That's just how matches work. If you don't claim it, it stays with them. I went home, opened the app on my phone, and changed two to four. It took ninety seconds. Ninety seconds, three years late.

**[S5: The sting, then the twist]**

That night, I did the painful maths. If that two thousand seven hundred and sixty had been invested and grown at, say, seven percent a year, which is a hypothetical, not a promise, in thirty years it could be roughly twenty-one thousand dollars. That's the real cost of a lease-shaped decision. Not the payment. The opportunity. But here's the twist. The day I fixed it, my take-home dropped by about thirty-two dollars a paycheck, because those contributions come out before income tax. And I never missed it. I honestly couldn't tell you what that thirty-two dollars used to buy me. Probably a car wash, for a car I didn't even own.

**[S6: Fast-forward, what I check now]**

Today, same car, eight years older. I earn around a hundred thousand a year, and I always take the full match, plus more on top. My cousin Priya, the one who earns two hundred thousand and dresses like she's off to hike a mountain, maxes hers out every year. Uncle Gary told me his retirement plan is "sell the boat." Everyone's different. But the match works the same for everyone who's offered one. It's the first money you're owed. So here's what I check now, and what I wish I'd checked at 25. One, your match formula. It's in the plan documents, not the vibes. Two, your vesting schedule, because some companies make you stay a few years before the match is fully yours. Three, next year's limit. For 2027, the actuarial firm Milliman projects twenty-five thousand five hundred dollars, up from twenty-four thousand five hundred. The IRS hadn't confirmed it at the end of October, so check the official number. You don't have to max it out. You just don't want to skip the free part.

**[S7: The handoff and close]**

If I could go back, I wouldn't lecture him about coffee. I'd just hand him that folder, open to page six. Before you cut a single thing, collect what you're already owed. The match. The HSA money. The stuff in the folder. Twenty-five-year-old me wanted to look rich. Twenty-eight-year-old me just wanted to stop missing free money. Honestly? The second guy was richer. Next week: the month three bills hit me at once, and why, for the first time, I didn't panic.
